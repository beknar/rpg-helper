# Tactics — Playing Both Sides at Their Best

The user asked for **the best tactics each side could command**. That
last clause is doing real work: it is not "the best tactics that exist",
it is the best tactics *that combatant* could find, given what it knows
and what it is capable of.

A simulation where a zombie plays like a general is not a better
simulation. It is a broken one.

## The Precedence Rule

When two sources disagree about what a creature does, this order wins:

1. **What the user said.** "Run it as if they charge straight in."
2. **The vault's `## Tactics` section.** Where it says what the monster
   does, that is the author's intent and it **outranks your own
   optimisation**. Coverage is uneven between vaults and often within
   one — some creatures carry a full doctrine, many carry nothing.
3. **The stat block's own design.** A block with Pack Tactics wants
   allies adjacent; a block with a recharge breath weapon wants targets
   clustered. The designer already encoded a doctrine.
4. **General optimal play**, gated by Intelligence — below.

**Where `## Tactics` says "Not described in the source"**, you are at
step 3 and 4, and should say so in the report.

## Intelligence Gates Tactical Quality

This is the fidelity rule that separates a simulation from a damage
calculator. Use the creature's actual Intelligence score.

| INT | How it fights |
|---|---|
| **1–2** (animal, ooze, most undead) | Attacks the nearest or the thing that just hurt it. No focus fire, no target selection, walks through obvious hazards, never retreats, never uses cover |
| **3–5** (beast-cunning) | Attacks the weakest-looking or most wounded adjacent target. Basic self-preservation. Will flee if badly hurt and the block allows it |
| **6–9** (ordinary humanoid) | Concentrates fire on one target, uses cover, focuses a spellcaster **once it has seen them cast**, retreats when losing |
| **10–13** (trained) | Opens well, protects its own casters, uses terrain, readies actions, targets concentration deliberately |
| **14+** (commander, fiend, lich) | Everything above, plus action-economy management, ambush setup, saving Legendary Resistance for the save that matters, and retreating with a plan |

**Undead are the common trap.** A skeleton has Intelligence 6 and can
be tactical; a zombie has Intelligence 3 and cannot. A ghoul at
Intelligence 13 can. Check the block rather than assuming "undead means
mindless".

## What Each Side Can Know

**Neither side has read the other's stat block.** Play to information,
not to omniscience.

**At the start**, a combatant knows what it can see: rough size, armour,
obvious weapons, whether something is glowing. It does **not** know hit
points, AC, resistances, immunities or spell lists.

**Knowledge is earned in play:**

- A creature that resists a hit visibly — "the blade bites and barely
  marks it" — teaches the party after **one** attack.
- A spellcaster is identifiable once they cast something obvious.
- A monster learns who hurt it most, and reacts.

**A PC with the right knowledge skill may know more**, and the sheet
says which skills they have. A wizard with Arcana against an aberration
is entitled to a check. Roll it, and let the result gate the tactics.

**Do not let either side act on information it has not earned.** A party
that opens by dumping radiant damage on the one creature vulnerable to
it, before anyone has cast a thing, is not playing well — it is
cheating, and it produces a verdict the GM cannot trust.

## Principles That Actually Decide 5e Fights

Apply these for any combatant with the Intelligence to use them.

**Focus fire.** The single most important tactic in the system. A
monster at 1 hit point deals full damage; a dead one deals none.
Concentrating on one target removes actions from the enemy's economy.
This is why identical creatures are tracked individually.

**Action economy is the war.** Every effect that removes a turn —
stun, paralysis, banishment, a failed save against *hold person* —
is worth more than its damage. Count turns, not just hit points.

**Break concentration.** One hit on a concentrating caster forces a save
at DC 10 or half the damage. Killing a *bless* or a *spirit guardians*
mid-fight swings more than a round of attacks, and a creature at
Intelligence 10+ should go looking for it.

**Hold the big resource for the moment it wins.** A dragon that breathes
on one target in round one wasted it. A cleric who burns the 3rd-level
slot on healing in round two may not have it when someone actually
drops.

**Positioning is damage.** Opportunity attacks, avoiding clusters
against area effects, forcing a melee-only monster to spend turns
walking. A monster with 40 feet of speed and no ranged attack can be
kited by a party that understands this — and an Intelligence 6 monster
will walk into it every time while an Intelligence 14 one will not.

**Retreat is a legitimate outcome.** If a creature's `## Tactics` says
it flees, or it is intelligent and clearly losing, it leaves. Record it
as a retreat, not a kill, and say what it would do next.

## Playing the PCs Well Without Playing Them Perfectly

**Use everything on the sheet.** Reactions, per-rest features and
consumables are the most-forgotten resources and they change outcomes.
Warding Flare, Channel Divinity, Second Wind, Relentless Endurance, a
potion of healing in the equipment list — all of it is available and all
of it should be spent when it is the right play.

**Prepared spells are what the sheet says.** Do not swap a PC's prepared
list to whatever would counter this monster. That is the single easiest
way to produce a fraudulently good result. If a different preparation
would change the verdict, **say so in "What would change it"** — that is
genuinely useful to a GM, and it is honest in a way that quietly
re-preparing is not.

**Party coordination is fair.** PCs talk, and a party may open with a
plan — the fighter takes the front, the wizard holds an area spell for
the cluster. That is how real tables play.

**Do not play PCs as flawless.** Perfect play produces a ceiling, not an
expectation. If you resolve every borderline decision optimally, say so
in the report, because the GM's real table will not, and the answer they
need is what happens at a real table.

## Stating Your Assumptions

The tactical choices **are** the simulation. Two competent runs of the
same fight can differ entirely on whether the monsters focus the cleric.

So in the report, state plainly:

- The **opening move** each side made, and why.
- Any **judgement call** that plausibly went the other way.
- Which `## Tactics` sections you followed, and where the vault was
  silent and you improvised.

A GM reading the report should be able to disagree with a specific
decision and know exactly which one to change.
