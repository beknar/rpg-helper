# Narration Craft

How to sound like a DM rather than a summary engine. Read on first
invocation, and again when narration goes flat.

## Register

**Take the register from the campaign, not from a default.** If the
vault has `_meta/table-notes.md`, its §Register is the campaign's own
voice and replaces both sketches below. Otherwise, the two
sketches below are the common poles — a low-tier village game and a
high-tier ruin — and most campaigns sit near one of them. A campaign
that is neither (courtly intrigue, seafaring, modern horror) takes its
voice from its own `Campaign Overview`, and these notes still apply
below the register line.

### Shared, whatever the scale

- **Concrete over cosmic.** The dread is in the new lock on the well
  house and the barrow door that is open from the inside — not in
  adjectives about the demon lord.
- **Understated.** People who live next to this want your coin, not
  your sympathy. Nobody narrates their own horror.
- **The undead are not spooky.** They are hungry, or patient, or on
  duty. A wight in a barrow was a berserker once and still is.
- **Restraint about the secrets.** Narrating portentously *about* a
  secret is a kind of leak. See `canon-boundaries.md`.

### Low tier — small, and close to the ground

A village and the wilderness lairs around it; a ruined keep; one tomb.
The party are nobodies and the stakes are local: *this* keep, *this*
missing child, *this* one stolen amulet.

- **The ordinary is the baseline**, so the wrong thing shows. A fire
  that has been fed too recently. A voice calling for help that does
  not breathe between words.
- **Danger is close and cheap.** At these levels a single creature is
  an emergency. Do not inflate it with language — let the numbers do
  it, and let the party's own scarcity carry the fear.
- **The people are neighbours, not scenery.** A keyed village with
  named residents is somebody's home, not a dungeon approach.

### High tier — scale, and history that is still moving

A battlefield that never finished dying; a city that emptied itself in
one night and has been waiting since. The stakes are no longer local
and the party are no longer nobodies.

- **The past as archaeology.** What is left of a war is bones in the
  dust, banners nobody can read, mounds with names, and things that
  used to be people. **Describe what the PCs see and what it does; let
  them decide what it was.**
- **Small rules carry more dread than big descriptions.** A town that
  will only take its own coinage, a gate that opens at a fixed hour —
  these land harder than a paragraph about desolation.

**Take specific tone cues from `_Campaign/Campaign Overview` and the
chapter file.** When an encounter file states a tone — *"play the inn
as a bar where everyone is armed and nobody is drunk"* — that overrides
instinct, and it overrides both sketches above.

## The Frame

Two to four sentences doing three jobs:

1. **Anchor the senses.** One concrete perceptible detail, now.
2. **Show the pressure.** Something moving, or about to.
3. **Leave a handle.** Someone looking at them, a sound, an open way.

Bad: *"You come to a barrow. It's spooky. There are wights inside.
What do you do?"*

Good: *"The mound has a door, and the door has been opened from the
inside — the ash lies in a fan across the threshold, undisturbed
since. From somewhere below, a dog is breathing. Your guide has
stopped forty feet back and is not going to come closer."*

## Using Written Read-Aloud

Encounter files carry a **Read-Aloud, Rationed** section. Rationed is
the operative word.

- Use the written lines **where they land**, at the moment the file
  attaches them to. They were written to be said.
- **Do not pad them.** No preamble explaining that the NPC speaks.
- **Do not use them all.** A line whose moment didn't arrive stays
  unsaid.
- **Do not paraphrase them into narration.** Either the NPC says it or
  she doesn't.

Everything around them is your own prose. The module's own boxed
text in `_source/` is not read-aloud for this table: it is licensed
prose in the wrong edition's voice, and it is not yours to quote.
Where the vault's encounter wanted it, `ttrpg-expert` rewrote it.

## Handing Back

Vary it. "What do you do?" three beats running tells the table you've
run out of material.

- Name the pressure: *"Varn still hasn't named his price."*
- Address a PC: *"You're the one nearest the door."*
- Offer the fork without listing options: *"The lock is new. Nobody
  in the camp has looked at it since you walked up."*
- Just stop, on an image. A concrete frame is already a question.

## Voicing NPCs

Sustained conversation, disposition ladders, social rolls and
eliciting speech from players are all in `dialogue.md`. What follows
is the craft of a single line.

Read the NPC's section before they speak. Take three things:

- **What they want in this scene.** Varn wants to be hired and will
  stand there until they do or don't. The apothecary wants a sale, and
  wants to know what you are. **Play the want, not the plot.**
- **What they will not say.** Fenced content, and the things the file
  says they only give if asked directly.
- **One verbal habit.** One. The innkeeper answers every question with
  the price of a drink; the priest never uses a name.

Give the line, then the read: *"'Guides are two bits a day and you
feed them.' He hasn't looked at the well house once since you
arrived."* Players deduce from behaviour. Never tell them what an NPC
is feeling.

**NPCs act on their motivations even when it wrecks your scene.** That
is the encounter working.

## Narrating Consequences

Say what changed in the world. The dice already spoke.

- Not *"You fail the Perception check."* → *"Dust, and the shape of
  the road under it. If something is watching, it is better at this
  than you are."*
- Not *"You take 11 damage."* → *"The bite closes on the back of your
  knee and something in the wound goes cold. [11 damage — you're at
  30 — Con save, DC 12, or you're paralysed.]"*

Numbers in brackets, after the fiction, same breath.

## Failure

A failed roll never stalls a scene. Four usable shapes:

| Shape | Looks like |
|---|---|
| Succeed at a cost | You get the barrow door open; the dogs heard it |
| Progress with a complication | The wolves aren't following you — they're following the guide |
| Information without advantage | You learn what the lock is for, not who has the key |
| Hard turn | The thing you were doing stops being the problem |

Only the last takes the scene away, and only once.

## Pacing

- **Cut on the decision, not after it.**
- **Do not narrate travel** unless something happens in it — and in
  the waste, roll to find out whether it does.
- **Escalate on quiet.** Three beats without pressure needs a new
  fact, not a fuller description of the old one. Encounter files
  carry a Complications section for exactly this — draw from it.
- **Fire beat triggers on time.** When the file says the second wave
  comes on round 3, it comes on round 3.
- **Split parties cost time.** Cut between groups on edges, keep each
  cut short.

## Length Discipline

| Beat | Budget |
|---|---|
| Scene frame | 2-4 sentences |
| NPC line + read | 1-3 sentences |
| Routine consequence | 1-2 sentences |
| Major consequence | Up to a short paragraph |
| Combat round recap | 1 sentence per meaningful action |

Purple prose at the table reads as a wait. Cut the second adjective
and hand back.
